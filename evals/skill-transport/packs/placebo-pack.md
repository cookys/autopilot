# Notes on the formation of sedimentary rock

Sedimentary rock forms from material that has been transported, deposited, and slowly bound
together over long spans of time. The story usually begins far from where the finished rock
will sit. Weathering breaks a parent rock apart: water works into cracks and freezes,
temperature swings expand and contract the surface, and mild acids in rainwater dissolve
certain minerals outright. The loosened fragments range from boulders down to particles too
fine to see, and each size travels differently once a river, a glacier, or the wind picks it
up.

Transport sorts the material almost without meaning to. A fast mountain stream carries coarse
gravel and rolls it along the bed, rounding the edges as the pieces knock against one
another. Where the water slows — at a bend, a lake, the mouth of a valley — the heaviest
grains drop first, then sand, then silt, and finally the finest clay settles only where the
water is nearly still. Because of this, a single deposit often shows a gradation from coarse
at the bottom to fine at the top, a pattern that later reveals how the current behaved on the
day the layer was laid down.

Deposition builds the layers one atop another. Each flood, each season, each storm can leave
its own bed, and over centuries the beds stack into thick sequences. The boundary between two
beds records a pause or a change: a shift in the current, a dry spell, a sudden influx of new
material. Ripple marks, mud cracks, and the tracks of animals are sometimes preserved on
these surfaces, frozen in place when the next layer buried them gently enough to keep the
detail intact.

Burial and time turn loose sediment into stone. As new material piles on, the weight squeezes
water out of the pore spaces and packs the grains tighter. Minerals dissolved in the
remaining water — commonly silica, calcite, or iron oxides — crystallise in the gaps and
cement the grains to their neighbours. The result depends on what was buried: sand becomes
sandstone, mud becomes shale or mudstone, and the calcium-rich remains of shells and coral
become limestone. The colours follow the chemistry, with iron lending reds and yellows and
organic matter darkening a rock toward grey or black.

What makes these rocks valuable to a reader of landscapes is that they keep their order. The
oldest layer lies at the bottom and the youngest at the top, unless later movement has tilted
or overturned the whole sequence. A cliff face therefore reads a little like a stack of pages,
each one a record of the conditions at the surface when it formed. Fossils sharpen the
account, since particular organisms lived in particular intervals, and a familiar fossil in a
layer ties that layer to the same interval wherever it appears.

None of this happens quickly. A layer a few centimetres thick may represent thousands of
years of patient accumulation, and a tall cliff can stand for tens of millions. The slow pace
is the point: sedimentary rock is less a single event than an archive, assembled grain by
grain, that patient study can read back into the history of a place long after the rivers and
seas that made it have gone.
