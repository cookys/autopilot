# Garden Rota Handbook

Seedlings that were started indoors are hardened off for a week in the cold frame before they go into the ground. Compost is turned every second Saturday, and the thermometer reading is written on the whiteboard beside the bins. Tools are hung on the outlined shapes painted on the shed wall so that a missing spade is noticed at a glance. Tomatoes are staked on the day they are planted, never later, since the roots of a grown plant do not forgive a post driven beside them. Beans and peas are sown in succession every three weeks until midsummer, which keeps the picking table busy into autumn. The herb spiral by the gate is cut back lightly and often; mint is kept in its own sunken bucket to stop it travelling. Surplus produce goes into the blue crates by the gate, and whatever is left on Sunday evening is taken to the food bank. Slugs are collected by torchlight after rain, and the bucket is emptied far from the beds, past the hedge. New members are shown the hose reels, the tap key cupboard and the first aid box on their first morning. The wheelbarrow with the flat tyre is labelled and stays by the gate until the pump has been returned from the neighbour. In frost warnings the tender plants are covered with fleece at dusk and uncovered again once the sun is on the beds. The notice board lists the sowing calendar by month, with a column for what can still be planted under cover. Visitors are welcome at the open afternoon on the first Saturday of each month, and children may water the sunflower row. Paths between the beds are kept to a width a wheelbarrow can pass, and bark chip is topped up each spring. Water butts are scrubbed once a year in March, when the first warm days make it pleasant work for a small team. Seed packets are stored in a biscuit tin with a silica gel sachet, sorted by the month in which they should be sown. The allotment association meets in the village hall on the last Thursday of the month, and minutes are posted on the door. Pumpkins are given a whole bed to themselves and a generous handful of well rotted manure under each mound. Strawberry runners are pegged down in July to make next year's plants, and the oldest plants are lifted after three seasons. A shared logbook in the shed records first and last frost dates, the first ripe tomato and the largest courgette of the year. Broken canes are not thrown away: the short ones label the rows and the long ones are tied into wigwams for the climbers. The bee hotel on the south fence is checked in winter, and any damp or mouldy tubes are replaced with fresh ones. Weeds are pulled before they set seed, and the roots of bindweed are lifted whole and left to dry on the path. Onion sets go in during March, and the tops are bent over by hand when the leaves begin to yellow in late summer. Gloves of every size live in the box by the door, and anyone may borrow a pair provided they are washed before returning. The pond is left alone from spring until the tadpoles have grown legs, and the netting is only lifted for a short time. Beetroot, carrots and parsnips are thinned in two passes, the second when the thinnings are big enough to eat. Leaves are collected in the autumn into a wire cage to rot down for two years and become the best soil improver on the plot. The key to the shed is kept in a combination box, and the code is changed each January and told to members in person. The community garden at the end of the lane is watered by volunteers on a rota that is pinned to the shed door. Each bed has a small painted stone with a number, and the stone is turned face down once the bed has been watered for the day. Rain barrels are filled first, because tap water in the dry weeks is metered and the association pays for every cubic metre.
